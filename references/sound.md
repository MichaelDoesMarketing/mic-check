# Sound

The layer under rhythm. What the words feel like in the mouth, regardless of what they mean.

Most of this is invisible to the script and most of it is worth less than breath, pocket, or room. Go here after those are fixed, not before.

## Consonants carry the hits

Stop consonants land. Continuants flow.

**Hard:** k, t, p, b, d, g, and the hard c. They interrupt the airflow completely, which is why they register as impacts.

**Soft:** s, f, v, m, n, l, r, w, th, sh. The air keeps moving. These blur into the words around them.

A sentence meant to land wants a stop consonant on its final stressed syllable. A sentence meant to flow wants continuants.

```
"It broke."    stops twice, lands like a dropped box
"It failed."   ends on a continuant, fades
"It's gone."   ends on a nasal, trails
"It's dead."   stops, lands
```

All four say roughly the same thing. They do not feel the same, and the difference has nothing to do with the dictionary.

Practical use: when a line is supposed to be the verdict of a paragraph and reads as merely true, look at its last consonant before you look at anything else. Swapping one word for a harder synonym often fixes a line that seemed to have a structural problem.

## Vowels carry the weight

Long vowels slow a line down. Short vowels speed it up. A sentence full of long vowels reads as grave whether or not the content is grave, which is how a sentence about expense reports ends up sounding like a eulogy.

Repeated vowel sounds across nearby stressed syllables bind words together and make a line feel composed rather than assembled. This is assonance, and in prose it should be barely noticeable. Two or three repeats in a short line is texture. Four is a tongue twister and the reader notices the writer instead of the point.

## Internal rhyme is texture, not rhyme

Full end rhyme in prose is almost always a mistake. It reads as doggerel and it makes a serious claim sound unserious.

Partial sound echo inside a sentence does something else entirely. It makes a line feel inevitable, like the words were found rather than chosen. The reader cannot say why one phrasing stuck and another did not.

```
"Built fast, broke faster."
"Nobody asked, so nobody answered."
"Big claim, small print."
```

None of these rhyme. All three repeat a sound structure across a pivot. That is the device, and it is the main reason some slogans survive and equivalent ones do not.

The limit: one per paragraph at most, and never in a sentence carrying a number or a caveat, because the sound pulls focus from the content and the content is the part that matters.

## Word length changes pace

Latinate words are long and abstract. Germanic words are short and concrete. English has both for almost everything, which means pace is a choice at the word level.

```
"We terminated the engagement."
"We quit."
```

A passage of entirely long words reads as slow and institutional even when the sentences are short. This is the sound of corporate writing and it is a syllable problem, not a vocabulary problem. Shortening sentences without shortening words does not fix it.

Mixed is the target. A long abstract word among short concrete ones carries emphasis precisely because it is slower.

## The mouth test

The one check worth running every time.

Read the line out loud at normal speaking pace. Not in your head, out loud, at the speed you would actually say it to someone.

Mark every place you:

- stumbled
- had to slow down to get the consonants out
- ran out of air
- heard your own voice go flat
- could not find the stress on first attempt

Each mark is a real defect. Readers do not stumble out loud, but they do stumble, and the stumble registers as the writing being hard without them knowing why.

A sentence you cannot say cleanly on the first try is a sentence to rewrite, and the one place where "it just felt wrong" is admissible evidence.

## What not to do with this

Do not optimize a passage for sound. Writing tuned for the mouth and not for the reader produces lines that are satisfying to say and impossible to remember, which is the failure mode of a lot of advertising.

Sound is a tiebreaker. When two phrasings carry the same meaning with the same precision, take the one that sounds better. When they do not carry the same meaning, meaning wins and the sound is irrelevant.
