# Rhythm by format

The correct rhythm is not a universal. Applying social-post timing to documentation makes the documentation worse. Check the format before rewriting anything.

Each entry below gives the shape that format wants, the failure it is prone to, and the axis that matters most.

---

## Social post, short

**Shape.** Front-loaded and top-heavy. The first line carries almost the entire job because it is the only line most people see. After that, short sentences with hard landings, one longer sentence somewhere in the middle for texture, and a last line that stops rather than concludes.

**Prone to.** An opener that explains instead of asserting. Also a closing line that summarizes what the reader just read, which is the single most common way a decent post ends badly.

**Axis that matters most.** Open. A post with a weak first line has no other axis.

---

## Social post, long

**Shape.** Paragraphs of one to three sentences with white space between them, because the white space is the breath. Rhythm operates at the paragraph level more than the sentence level: a long paragraph needs a one-line paragraph after it.

**Prone to.** Every paragraph being the same size, which produces a visual drone the reader sees before reading a word. Also the list-of-insights structure, where eight paragraphs all have identical shape.

**Axis that matters most.** Variance, measured across paragraphs rather than sentences.

---

## Landing page

**Shape.** Fragments are correct here and the script will not know that. Headlines want hard landings. Body copy wants short sentences that stack into a case rather than a story. Different sections want different rhythms on purpose, because the page is scanned in chunks and each chunk is its own passage.

**Prone to.** Hero copy with a long sentence in it. Also three benefit statements with identical construction, which reads as a template.

**Axis that matters most.** Landing. Nothing on a landing page should trail off.

---

## Email

**Shape.** Conversational, which means closer to how the sender actually talks than to how they write. Mostly medium sentences. Short opener, short closer. The sentence containing the ask should be the shortest sentence in the email and should land hard.

**Prone to.** A long first sentence of context before the point. Also softening the ask into a trailing construction, which is what makes an email feel like it did not ask for anything.

**Axis that matters most.** Open, then Landing on the ask specifically.

---

## Blog post or article

**Shape.** The format with the most room to move and the one where drone is most fatal, because the reader has an exit at every scroll. Sections should have different rhythms from each other. Argument sections run longer and more even. Example and story sections run shorter and more varied.

**Prone to.** Settling into a comfortable band by the third section and staying there to the end. Run the script on each section separately, because a whole-piece average will look fine while section four is a brick wall.

**Axis that matters most.** Variance, measured per section.

---

## Case study

**Shape.** Flat by nature and that is correct. The reader is evaluating, not being entertained. Even, medium sentences through the setup and method. The rhythm earns its variation only at the result, where a short hard-landing sentence after a stretch of even ones delivers the number.

**Prone to.** Overcorrection. A case study written with social-post energy reads as a brochure and loses the credibility the format exists to build.

**Axis that matters most.** Room, specifically around the result.

---

## Technical documentation

**Shape.** Even, short, predictable. The reader is scanning under pressure and looking for one thing. Rhythm should be invisible.

**Prone to.** Nothing this skill should fix. A drone score on documentation is usually a pass.

**Axis that matters most.** Breath, and only breath. A documentation sentence nobody can parse in one go is a real defect. Everything else should be left alone.

---

## Script, video, or anything spoken

**Shape.** The only format where the breath constraint is literal rather than inferred. Every breath group has to be sayable by the specific person saying it, at the pace they actually talk, with the stresses falling where a human would put them.

**Prone to.** Being written as prose. Written sentences are longer than spoken ones and a script that reads fine on the page will strand the performer.

**Axis that matters most.** Breath, and the threshold is tighter here. Flag anything over 15 syllables, not 20.

---

## Headline, subject line, tagline

**Shape.** Too short for the script to say anything useful. Measured in stresses rather than sentences: two, three, or four strong beats, with the strongest on the last one.

**Prone to.** Ending on an unstressed syllable, which makes a tagline feel unfinished for reasons the writer cannot locate.

**Axis that matters most.** None of them. Run the mouth test from `sound.md` instead and ignore the score.

---

## When the format is not on this list

Ask two questions.

**Is the reader under pressure?** Under pressure wants even and predictable. At leisure can take variation.

**Is the reader deciding or absorbing?** Deciding wants hard landings and short sentences at the decision points. Absorbing can carry long ones.

Those two answers get you most of the way to the right shape for anything.
