# Breath

The oldest constraint on language and the one writers ignore most, because typing has no lungs.

## What is actually being measured

Not words per sentence. Syllables per breath group.

A breath group is the stretch between places a reader can take air: commas, semicolons, colons, dashes, parentheses, and the end of the sentence. A long sentence built from short clauses is comfortable. A short sentence with no internal punctuation can be harder than a long one that breathes.

```
Forty-one words, six breath groups, easy:
"We shipped it on Friday, which was a mistake, because nobody was around
to watch the logs, and by Monday the queue had backed up so far that the
retries were timing out, which meant the retries were generating more
retries, and so on."

Twenty-six words, one breath group, hard:
"The fundamental issue with the current approach to customer segmentation
across the enterprise portfolio is that it assumes behavioral consistency
within demographic cohorts."
```

The second one is shorter and it suffocates. Word count would have told you the opposite.

## Where the numbers come from

Speech-production research gives usable figures, with real limits.

Kallay, Mayr and Redford (2019) recorded six American English speakers reciting a memorized passage and measured what happened at pauses. Mean utterance length before a breath intake was 9.25 syllables, standard deviation 6.56. The probability that a pause contained a breath rose with the length of the sentence before it: 87 percent after a twelve-syllable sentence against 64 percent after a six-syllable one. Articulation rate before a breath was 4.41 syllables per second.

Separately, a commonly cited typical rate for English is about 4 syllables per second, with one reported range of 3.3 to 5.9 across contexts.

So a comfortable spoken phrase is roughly two to three seconds of sound, somewhere around nine to twelve syllables, and by twelve the speaker is usually taking air.

**What this does not establish.** All of it is about speech out loud. None of these studies measured silent reading, subvocalization rate, or whether a reader's inner voice obeys the same limit. The sample above is six people reciting a memorized passage, which is not conversation and not reading. The connection between spoken breath groups and reading comfort is an inference this skill makes, not a finding it can cite.

Treat the thresholds below as a working calibration that produces good results, not as physiology. If someone asks whether the research proves silent readers need to breathe, the honest answer is no.

## The thresholds this skill uses

| Breath group | Reading |
|---|---|
| Under 12 syllables | Comfortable. Speakers take air around here |
| 12 to 20 | Fine. Most good prose lives in this range |
| 20 to 30 | Strained. Works once in a passage, not twice |
| Over 30 | Nobody says this out loud. Split it |

The script flags any group over 20 and scores on what share of the passage is over that line, not on the single worst offender. One long sentence among short ones is a feature. Four in a row is suffocation.

## Why long sentences fail

Not because they are complicated. Readers handle complicated.

They fail because the reader commits working memory to an unresolved clause and the resolution keeps not arriving. Every additional word is a small cost paid against a promise. Past a certain length the reader stops paying and skims to the period, which means they receive the shape of the sentence and none of its content.

The tell is that readers cannot tell you what a long sentence said. They can usually tell you how they felt about reading it.

## Fixing it

In order of how often it works.

**Punctuate.** The cheapest fix and usually enough. The sentence is fine. It has no handholds. Adding two commas can take a group from 28 syllables to three groups of 9.

**Cut the preamble.** Long clauses are usually long at the front. "What I think is interesting about this is that" is nine syllables of throat-clearing before the sentence starts.

**Split at the conjunction.** Most overlong sentences are two sentences joined by "and," "but," "which," or "because." The join is the seam. Cut there and the two halves usually both improve.

**Move the subject earlier.** A sentence that holds its subject in reserve makes the reader carry everything before it. Front-load and the breath groups fall out naturally.

**Lose the qualifications.** Hedges are where sentences go to get long. Three qualifications in a clause means the writer has not decided what they think. Deciding is shorter.

## The counterweight

Everything short is its own failure and the script will not catch it, because short sentences score well on breath.

A passage of nothing but short sentences reads as breathless rather than comfortable. It also reads as condescending, because the reader feels managed. Long sentences do real work: they hold several things in relation to each other, they build, and they create the pressure that makes the next short sentence land.

The target is a passage where the long ones are long on purpose and survive being said out loud.
